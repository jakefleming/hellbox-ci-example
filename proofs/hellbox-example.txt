Titivillus keeps a sack. Every letter you drop, every word your hand skips while your mind wanders off somewhere warm, he picks it up and puts it in the sack. This is his whole job. Hell runs on paperwork, and the paperwork runs on your mistakes.
Ask any medieval scribe. Twelve hours bent over vellum, candle smoke in your eyes, wrist gone numb at the third psalm, and one demon at your elbow working a quota. A thousand and one sacks a day. That was the number. Fill them with botched abbreviations and swapped vowels, with the name of God spelled backwards, then haul them below to be read into the record. Your typos, entered into evidence against you.
He gets his first writeup around 1285, a friar's tract on penitence, and the friar quotes the demon's own motto: fragmina verborum titivillus colligit horum. Titivillus collects the fragments of these words. Understand what that means. The oldest job description in publishing belongs to quality assurance, and quality assurance works for the other side.
Monks drew him in the margins so the novices would know what was eating their afternoons. Fat, grinning, sack over one shoulder, peering over the copyist's arm like a foreman. Some houses said he also collected syllables mumbled during prayers, the shortcuts, the swallowed amens, the psalms taken at a jog. The lazy tongue and the lazy hand feed the same demon. A nun in one story dies with her sins recorded on a parchment so long the demon carrying it has to fold it double. Recordkeeping was always the punishment. The fire is just the filing system.
Then the press came, and the demon got a promotion. A scribe makes one mistake at a time. A compositor standing at a type case makes his mistake four hundred times before lunch, and every copy signs the confession. In 1631 the royal printers of London set the seventh commandment and left out the word not. Thou shalt commit adultery, said the Bible, one thousand copies, king's imprimatur on the title page. They called it the Wicked Bible. The printers were fined into ruin. Somewhere below, a sack got very heavy.
The printers knew who they were dealing with, even if they stopped saying his name. They kept a box under the bench for type too battered to trust, worn shoulders, cracked serifs, an e that printed like a c on its third drink. The hellbox. Broken letters went in, got hauled to the foundry, melted, and came back as new type, clean and innocent, ready to fail again. Titivillus approved of the arrangement. He has always preferred a system with a return policy.
He never retired. He relocated. Out of the scriptorium, into the type case, into the Linotype, where a slipped finger printed etaoin shrdlu in the morning paper under a headline about the harvest. Into phototype, where tight was not tight enough until the letters touched. Into the autocorrect, which fixes the word you wanted into the word it wanted. He is the r n that reads as m at nine points. He is the double space after a period that survives four rounds of edits. He is the straight quote posing as an apostrophe in a wedding invitation, the kern between A and V that nobody fixed because nobody printed the word AVAIL big enough to see it.
So you proof. You print the page bigger than any reader will ever see it and you read it backwards, one word at a time, out loud, like a spell, because forward reading is how he gets you. Forward, your brain fixes the errors for free and files nothing. You check the spacing with fence posts, HHAHOHOAOO, nnanonoaoo, letter by letter between the controls, because texture lies at a distance and confesses up close. You set the same paragraph six ways and stare at the gray of it. Even color is a page with nothing to hide. Blotches mean the demon has been through.
A proof with no errors is not a proof. It is a specimen, a brochure, a handshake. A proof exists to be bled on. Every circle you draw around a collapsed counter or a crashed descender is a letter taken back out of the sack, and the sack does not like giving things back.
You will not catch every error. That is the arrangement, and it is older than print. You catch what you can, and the sack gets the rest.
